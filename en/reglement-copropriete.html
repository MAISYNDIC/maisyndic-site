The co-ownership rules

They set the ground rules for the building. They state what is private and what is common, how everyone may use it, how the building is run and what share of the charges falls to each unit.

Key points

  • Every co-ownership must have co-ownership rules.
  • They set the intended use of the units, the use of the common parts, how the building is administered and each unit's share of the charges.
  • They can only impose restrictions justified by the building's intended use.
  • They also bind tenants and occupants.
  • Amending them usually requires a double majority, sometimes unanimity.

What do the co-ownership rules contain?

The law gives them four roles: defining the intended use of the private parts and the common parts, setting the conditions for their enjoyment, laying down the rules for administering the common parts and setting each unit's share of the charges. They often come with a division schedule, which numbers and describes the units, and a schedule allocating the charges.

Any clause unrelated to these purposes is deemed unwritten: it does not apply, even if it appears in the document.

Private parts and common parts

Private parts are those reserved for the exclusive use of one co-owner; they belong to that co-owner. Common parts are those used by all the co-owners or by several of them; they belong to those co-owners jointly, in undivided shares. No one can transfer their share of the common parts separately from their unit.

If the rules say nothing, or contradict themselves, the law presumes the following, among others, to be common:

  • the land, courtyards, parks and gardens, and access roads;
  • the structure and the common equipment, including pipes that run through the homes;
  • chimney breasts, flues and chimney tops;
  • rooms for shared services, passageways and corridors;
  • terraces, balconies, balustrades and railings, except the surface covering of terraces and balconies that cannot be seen from outside.

Partitions between two units that are not part of the structure are presumed to be jointly owned by those two units.

What the rules can and cannot impose

The rules can regulate how the building is used, but they cannot impose on co-owners any restriction that is not justified by the building's intended use, as it results from the deeds, the building's characteristics or its location.

Likewise, the general meeting cannot, by any majority, force a co-owner to change the intended use of their unit or the conditions of enjoyment set for it in the rules.

Who must comply with them?

The rules bind all co-owners and anyone deriving rights from them, including tenants and all occupants. If you let your home, pass on to your tenant the rules that concern them, such as the use of the common parts.

To be binding on a new buyer, the rules must have been registered with the mortgage registry responsible for the building. The same applies to each amendment: an amendment that has been voted but not registered cannot be enforced against someone who later buys a unit.

Amending the co-ownership rules

The majority depends on what changes:

  • use, enjoyment and administration of the common parts: a majority of co-owners holding at least three quarters of the votes (article 17);
  • allocation of charges: unanimity as a rule, with the exceptions set by law (see our guide to charges);
  • intended use or conditions of enjoyment of a unit: not without the agreement of the co-owner concerned.

The draft amendment is attached to the meeting notice. Once voted, it must be registered with the mortgage registry, which in practice is done through a notary. The majorities are explained in detail in our guide to the general meeting.

No rules? They must be drawn up

A co-ownership that has none must draw up co-ownership rules and have them published. The general meeting adopts the text by the article 17 majority. Failing that, any co-owner may ask the court to draw them up.

Where can you find your co-ownership rules?

You should normally have received them when you bought your unit, and the syndic keeps them in the building's records. At mäisyndic, they are available at any time in the online portal, alongside the minutes, statements and contracts. When we take over a building, we apply the allocation keys they set out; if an amendment becomes necessary, we prepare the draft resolution for the meeting and liaise with the notary.

This guide summarises the amended law of 16 May 1975 on the status of co-ownership of built property and the Grand-Ducal regulation of 13 June 1975 implementing it, as currently in force. Your co-ownership rules may contain more detailed provisions. Only the official texts are legally binding: law of 16 May 1975 and Grand-Ducal regulation of 13 June 1975 on Legilux.
